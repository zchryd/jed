Jedediah Smith Redwoods · Smith River · Del Norte Coast

Field guide: what lives where, and how to know it.

Eight habitats, one river. Walk from the tallest forest on Earth to a pitcher-plant bog to a tidepool in a single day — this guide tells you what you're looking at in each one.

How to trust this page: species facts here are standard, well-established regional natural history, cross-checked against official park and wildlife-agency pages; everything involving safety or rules got an extra adversarial review. Where something couldn't be confirmed to current official sources (exact goose counts, one trail's posted mileage), the wording says so. Two corrections that review made: it's the northern red-legged frog here (not the California species), and green sturgeon are at most rare visitors to the lower river — they don't spawn in the Smith.

ZONE 1

The river-flat giants — alluvial old growth

Stand here: Stout Grove (0.5-mi loop) · the Grove of Titans boardwalk · the campground's own river flat.

The very biggest redwoods grow on the flats, and it's no accident: every big flood (1955 and 1964 among them) lays down a fresh sheet of silt, and redwoods respond by growing a brand-new root system up into it — most trees would suffocate; redwoods get richer soil. The result is open, cathedral-like groves with almost no shrub layer: just huge spaced columns over a solid carpet of redwood sorrel and sword fern. Flood-prone ground also kills off the competition, which is why the flats are nearly pure redwood.

Coast redwood

Sequoia sempervirens · K'vsh-chu in Tolowa

Look for: soft, fibrous, deeply-ridged red-brown bark (up to a foot thick); flat, two-ranked needles on shade branches but short awl-like needles up top; cones startlingly small — grape-sized — for the tallest living thing on Earth. Sprouts in rings from burls and stumps: no other big conifer here does that.

All year

Redwood sorrel

Oxalis oregana

Look for: the shamrock carpet under the giants. Leaves fold down like little umbrellas within minutes in direct sun — watch a sunfleck move and the carpet moves with it. Pink-white flowers in spring.

Blooms Apr–Jun

Sword fern

Polystichum munitum

Look for: shoulder-high evergreen fountains everywhere; each leaflet has a little "sword hilt" lobe at its base. The default fern of the forest floor.

All year

Western trillium

Trillium ovatum

Look for: everything in threes — three leaves, three white petals that age to pink-purple. Never pick one: the plant can take years to recover.

Feb–May

Clintonia (bead lily)

Clintonia andrewsiana

Look for: broad glossy basal leaves, a stalk of deep-rose flowers in late spring, then metallic steel-blue berries by late summer — the bluest thing on the forest floor. Not edible.

May–Jun · berries Aug

Banana slug

Ariolimax columbianus

Look for: up to 8 inches of bright yellow to olive slug, out on damp mornings. The forest's recycler — it eats leaves, dung, and mushrooms and spreads seeds and spores. Its slime numbs the tongue of anything that licks it; don't be that animal.

Wet days, all year

Varied thrush

Ixoreus naevius

Listen for: a single eerie, buzzy whistled note held for a second, then a long pause, then another on a different pitch — the soundtrack of old growth. Looks like a robin wearing a black necklace band.

Best fall–spring

Pacific wren

Troglodytes pacificus

Listen for: an impossibly long, tumbling, tinkling song from a bird the size of a ping-pong ball, usually in root-wads and fern shadows near the ground.

Sings Mar–Jul

Wandering salamander

Aneides vagrans

Fun one: you probably won't see it — many live their whole lives 200+ feet up, in the fern mats and canopy soil that accumulate on big redwood limbs. A vertical world with its own residents.

Hidden, all year
ZONE 2

Upland forest — redwood, Douglas-fir, and friends

Stand here: Boy Scout Tree Trail · Mill Creek Trail · anywhere Howland Hill Road climbs.

Off the flats, the forest mixes. Slopes bring Douglas-fir, western hemlock, and grand fir in with the redwoods, plus a real shrub layer — this is where the rhododendrons put on their May–June show. Five big conifers look alike at first; bark and needles tell them apart in seconds.

Douglas-fir

Pseudotsuga menziesii

Look for: thick, deeply-furrowed corky gray-brown bark; soft needles all around the twig; and the giveaway cone — three-pointed "mouse tails" sticking out of every scale. No other cone here has them.

All year

Western hemlock

Tsuga heterophylla

Look for: the drooping top — the leader nods over like a whip. Tiny needles of mixed lengths give sprays a feathery, messy look; cones are small and papery. (Rogers called the 1828 giants "hemlock" — now you know why he was guessing.)

All year

Sitka spruce

Picea sitchensis

Look for: bark in thin, flaking potato-chip scales, and needles so stiff and sharp a handshake with a branch hurts. Loves the fog belt and river bottoms near the coast.

All year

Grand fir

Abies grandis

Look for: glossy flat needles combed neatly into two ranks, white-striped beneath; crush a few — citrus. True-fir cones stand upright on the branch and fall apart in place, so you'll rarely find a whole one on the ground.

All year

Port Orford cedar

Chamaecyparis lawsoniana

Look for: lacy flat sprays with white X-shaped marks on the underside; tiny round cones like soccer balls. A Smith River specialty — this drainage holds some of the last big stands untouched by the introduced root disease that's killing the species elsewhere. It spreads in mud: stay on trails, and arrive with clean boots.

All year

Tanoak · madrone · California bay

Notholithocarpus · Arbutus · Umbellularia

Quick IDs: tanoak — toothed leathery leaves, fuzzy acorn caps. Madrone — smooth orange-red bark peeling like sunburn. Bay — crush a leaf and your sinuses will report immediately.

All year

Pacific rhododendron

Rhododendron macrophyllum

Look for: big evergreen leaf rosettes and fist-sized pink flower trusses against redwood bark — the park's signature photo. Its cousin western azalea (deciduous, white-and-gold, honeysuckle-sweet smell) blooms in wetter spots.

May–Jun

Huckleberries, two ways

Vaccinium ovatum · V. parvifolium

Look for: evergreen huckleberry — small glossy dark leaves, blue-black berries late summer. Red huckleberry — bright green zig-zag twigs, translucent red berries, often growing straight out of old stumps.

Berries Jul–Sep

Marbled murrelet

Brachyramphus marmoratus

The old-growth secret: a seabird that fishes the ocean but nests on wide mossy redwood limbs, flying inland at dawn. Listen for high "keer" calls over the canopy at first light. It's also why rangers are fierce about crumbs: food scraps feed jays and ravens, and jays eat murrelet eggs. A crumb-free picnic is murrelet conservation.

Nests Apr–Sep

Northern spotted owl

Strix occidentalis caurina

Status: still here in the old growth, but declining as barred owls move in. A four-note hoot after dark ("who — who-who — whooo") is a good night. Report any sighting to a ranger; they track them.

Heard at night

Black bear & black-tailed deer

Reality check: deer are daily; bears are common but shy — you'll see claw-scarred trunks and torn logs more often than bears. The campground's bear lockers are the law for all food and toiletries. Mountain lions and bobcats live here too; sightings are rare gifts.

All year

Humboldt marten & fisher

Martes caurina humboldtensis · Pekania pennanti

The rarities: two house-cat-sized forest hunters of old growth. The coastal marten was thought extinct until rediscovered on the Six Rivers NF in 1996; a few hundred remain. Seeing either is lottery luck — but this forest is exactly what they need.

Nearly never seen
ZONE 3

The river — clear, cold, and alive

Stand here: the gravel bar below the campground · Hiouchi Bridge · the summer footbridge.

The Smith runs jade-green and absurdly clear because its bed is hard rock and its watershed is unlogged-enough, undammed, and steep — storms blow through it fast (it can rise from a summer trickle's 2,000 cfs to 50,000+ in days, and drop back just as fast). Winter is the drama season: big water, and the salmon and steelhead that made the river famous.

Chinook salmon

Oncorhynchus tshawytscha

See them: late fall–early winter, when big dark fish push upriver after the first storms. Watch quietly from the Hiouchi Bridge or gravel-bar edges; look for rolling backs in pools and V-wakes over shallows.

Oct–Dec

Coho salmon & steelhead

O. kisutch · O. mykiss

Know: the Smith is a stronghold for threatened coho (they favor the smaller tributaries like Mill Creek) and for winter steelhead — the wild run anglers whisper about, Dec–Mar. Coastal cutthroat hide under the alder banks all year.

Coho Nov–Jan · steelhead Dec–Mar

Pacific lamprey

Entosphenus tridentatus

Respect: ancient, jawless, eel-shaped — and a treasured food of the Tolowa Dee-ni' for millennia; the first written record of this river (June 20, 1828) is Tolowa traders selling "lamprey eels" to Jedediah Smith's clerk. Look for suction-scars on rocks and, in spring, lampreys wrestling stones to build nests.

Spawns spring

American dipper

Cinclus mexicanus

Look for: a slate-gray tennis ball of a bird doing knee-bends on a mid-river rock, then walking underwater. Where a dipper lives, the river is healthy.

All year

River otter · osprey · kingfisher · merganser

The river crew: otter families porpoising in quiet pools (dawn/dusk); ospreys hovering and crashing feet-first for fish in summer; the belted kingfisher's rattle; common merganser flotillas of striped chicks in June–July.

Spring–fall best

Pacific giant salamander

Dicamptodon tenebrosus

Look for: under cool creek-side wood (look, don't move the wood far, put it back): a foot-long, marbled-brown salamander — one of the few salamanders that can vocalize; a disturbed one may give a little bark-squeak.

All year, hidden

Rough-skinned newt

Taricha granulosa

Handle with care: pebbly brown back, traffic-cone-orange belly, strolling in the open like it owns the trail — because it does: its skin carries tetrodotoxin. Fine to watch; if you must move one off a road, use a leaf, don't touch your face, wash your hands. Never anywhere near mouths.

Most visible in rains
ZONE 4

The riverbank edge — alder and berry country

Stand here: the line where gravel bar meets forest, anywhere along the Stout Grove loop or River Beach.

Between water and redwood runs a narrow, sunlit strip that floods too often for conifers — so it belongs to fast movers: red alder (speckled gray bark, little woody "cones"), willow thickets, bigleaf maple draped in moss. This is berry and birdsong country, and in summer the busiest wildlife edge in the park.

Red alder

Alnus rubra

Look for: pale speckled bark (often lichen-white) and mini pine-cone catkins. Alders fix nitrogen — they're the riverbank's soil-builders after every flood.

All year

Salmonberry & thimbleberry

Rubus spectabilis · R. parviflorus

Look for: salmonberry — magenta flowers in earliest spring, then salmon-orange raspberries in June. Thimbleberry — huge soft maple-shaped leaves, white flowers, flat red caps that fall apart in your fingers.

Berries Jun–Aug

Red elderberry

Sambucus racemosa

Look for: frothy cream flower cones then bright-red berry clusters. Raw berries are mildly toxic (birds don't care). The Tolowa village inside this park — Chvn-su'lh-dvn, "Red Elderberry Place" — is named for this shrub; when you see one on the Hiouchi flat, you're seeing the reason for a name a thousand years older than the park.

Berries Jul–Aug

Poison oak & stinging nettle

Toxicodendron diversilobum · Urtica dioica

The edge's two gotchas: poison oak ("leaves of three, let it be" — glossy, scalloped, red in fall, bare stems in winter still potent) likes sunny edges and river bars, rarely deep redwood shade. Nettle lines damp banks; brush = sting. Long pants on brushy edges solve both.

All year
ZONE 5

Serpentine country — the botanical island

Stand here: Little Bald Hills Trail (the forest just stops) · Myrtle Creek Botanical Area off US-199.

Climb the Little Bald Hills and the tallest forest on Earth gives way, in a few hundred feet, to open stunted woodland on rust-red soil. That's serpentine — rock so high in magnesium and heavy metals, and so starved of calcium, that ordinary plants fail. What grows instead is a specialist flora found almost nowhere else, which is exactly why the Smith River country is a legend among botanists.

California pitcher plant

Darlingtonia californica

Look for: knee-high hooded green "cobras" with a forked tongue, standing in wet serpentine seeps. It eats insects: nectar lures them under the hood, false-window skylights confuse them, downward hairs do the rest. Find them in fen pockets in the Myrtle Creek / Little Bald Hills serpentine — and ask at the Gasquet ranger station for the current best roadside fen stop on 199.

Pitchers all year · flowers Jun

Reading serpentine

Look for: rusty-orange bare soil, glossy blue-green rock with a soapy feel, trees suddenly small and spaced (stunted pines and cypresses instead of redwoods), and spring wildflowers you won't have seen all trip. Botanists call these "islands"; treat them that way — stay on the trail, the soils scar for decades.

Wildflowers Apr–Jun

Why no redwoods here?

The lesson: redwoods are champions of the rich flats, not of hardship. On serpentine their advantage collapses and the tough specialists win — the whole competitive order of the forest, rewritten by chemistry underfoot.

ZONE 6

Dunes & lagoon — Tolowa Dunes and Lake Earl

Stand here: Tolowa Dunes State Park trailheads off Lake Earl Drive, 30 minutes from camp.

North of Crescent City the forest yields to a huge sweep of dunes wrapped around Lake Earl and Lake Talawa — one of California's largest coastal lagoon systems. The lagoon periodically breaches its sandbar to the sea and swings between fresh and brackish, which makes it a bird machine: rafts of waterfowl in winter, shorebird flocks on passage, raptors patrolling the dune grass. This is also storied ground — Yontocket and 'Ee-chuu-le' stand at its edges (see the Historical Map), so walk it as a guest.

Aleutian cackling goose

Branta hutchinsii leucopareia

The comeback: a small goose with a white neck-ring that fell to a few hundred birds in the mid-1900s (island foxes ate them on their Alaskan breeding islands) — Del Norte's pastures were their historic spring staging ground, and spring skeins here were part of one of conservation's great recoveries. These days the biggest flocks winter farther south in Humboldt; in spring, look up anyway.

Spring

Waterfowl & raptors

Scan for: wigeon, pintail and teal rafts on the lagoon in winter; white egrets stalking the edges; northern harriers coursing low over dune grass with their white rump-patch flashing; peregrines putting everything to flight.

Fall–spring

Dune plants

Look for: mats of pink sand verbena and silvery beach sagewort holding the dunes together, with shore pine behind. Stay off the vegetated dune faces — those mats are decades old.

Blooms May–Aug
ZONE 7

Rocky shore & tidepools

Stand here: Enderts Beach (best all-around) · Pebble Beach · below Point St. George — always on a falling low tide.

Check a tide table (Crescent City station), arrive an hour before low, and read the shore in stripes: barnacles highest, then mussel beds with ochre stars, then anemone-and-surfgrass gardens at the waterline. Touch gently with a wet finger, pry nothing loose, and never turn your back on the ocean — this coast's sneaker waves are genuinely dangerous.

Ochre sea star

Pisaster ochraceus

Look for: stout five-armed stars in purple or orange clamped to mussel beds. The population is rebuilding after the 2013+ sea-star wasting epidemic — finding several is good news you can see.

Low tide

Giant green anemone

Anthopleura xanthogrammica

Look for: emerald flowers the size of your palm in the lowest pools (the green is partly solar — algae living in its tissues). A gentle wet-finger touch gets a sticky handshake; that's its hunting grip.

Low tide

Chitons, hermit crabs, sculpins

Look closer: oval armor-plated chitons welded to rock (including the brick-red giant Pacific chiton); every turban snail shell may be walking (hermit crab); camouflaged sculpins darting in the pools. Harbor seals watch you from the water; give hauled-out seals 100 yards.

Low tide

Gray whales

Eschrichtius robustus

From the bluffs: heart-shaped blows, southbound Dec–Jan (mothers north with calves Mar–May). Point St. George and Crescent Beach Overlook are the local grandstands; the Klamath River Overlook (40 min south) is the famous one.

Dec–Jan · Mar–May
ZONE 8

Reading the forest — old growth vs. second growth

Stand here: Howland Hill Road passes through both — watch the forest change as you drive.

Once you know the tells, you can read a forest's biography from the roadside. Old growth: trunks of wildly different sizes, big irregular canopy gaps, huge downed logs in every stage of decay, standing dead spires, and that open, mossy, layered feel. Second growth: even-aged trunks all about the same size standing close and straight, thin uniform canopy, little large wood on the ground.

Fairy rings

Look for: a perfect circle of redwoods — they sprouted from the living root crown of one logged or fallen giant, whose ghost is the empty center. The genetic individual may be thousands of years old even where every visible trunk is young.

—

Nurse logs & goosepens

Look for: straight rows of trees that all germinated on one long-gone fallen log (some still stand on "stilts" where the log rotted away), and fire-hollowed living trunks big enough to stand in — settlers penned geese in them, hence the name. Both are old-growth signatures: recovery measured in centuries.

—

Fog, the summer river

Why mornings drip: summer rain here is fog — redwood needles comb water from marine fog and rain it onto their own roots (a meaningful share of the forest's summer water). It's why the giant groves hug the fog belt, and why a "dry" July morning under the canopy isn't.

Jun–Sep mornings
ZONE 9

The fungus year — and the flowers that pretend

Stand here: any trail, October through January, after the rains return.

Fall flips a switch: the first soaking storms wake a mushroom flora the region is famous for. In the state park you're a photographer, not a forager — collecting anything is prohibited — and that makes the park a great place to learn them before deciding whether foraging (on national-forest land, under its rules) is ever for you.

Pacific golden chanterelle

Cantharellus formosus

Look for: egg-yolk gold trumpets with blunt, forking ridges (not true knife-edge gills) running down the stem; smells faintly of apricot. The classic of the coastal forest.

Oct–Jan

King bolete · matsutake · candy cap

The other celebrities: king bolete — a burger-bun cap over a spongy pore layer, no gills at all. Matsutake — white, firm, smells of cinnamon-and-pine. Candy cap — small brown cap that smells of maple syrup as it dries. All revered, all with dangerous imitators — enjoy by eye.

Oct–Jan

Turkey tail & artist's conk

Trametes versicolor · Ganoderma applanatum

On every log: turkey tail — banded fans in fuzzy rainbow browns. Artist's conk — a hard gray shelf whose white underside bruises brown at a touch (hence the name; look for old ones bearing decades-old scratched drawings — and leave them be).

All year

Fly agaric

Amanita muscaria

The postcard: the red cap with white flecks, under spruce and pine near the coast. Beautiful, photogenic, toxic. Admire; don't sample.

Oct–Dec

Death cap & destroying angel

Amanita phalloides · A. ocreata

The reason for all the warnings: plain-looking pale mushrooms — greenish-tan or pure white cap, skirt on the stem, cup at the base, often near oaks — whose toxin destroys livers days after a pleasant-tasting meal. They grow in this region. The rule that keeps people alive: never eat any wild mushroom without an expert identification in hand.

Fall–winter

The flowers that pretend to be fungi

Allotropa · Hemitomes · Corallorhiza · Pityopus

Look for: ghostly leafless stems on the dark forest floor — candy-striped sugarstick, pinkish gnome plant, spotted coralroot orchid, pale California pinefoot. They're true flowering plants with no chlorophyll: they tap the underground fungal network that links the trees, and surface only to bloom. The redwood forest's strangest wildflowers.

May–Aug
Safety, the short list
  • Mushrooms: never eat wild finds without expert ID — death caps and destroying angels grow here and taste fine. Too late to un-eat one; poison control: 1-800-222-1222.
  • Rough-skinned newts carry tetrodotoxin — look, don't handle; wash hands. The yellow-spotted millipede advertises with almond smell (cyanide) — same policy.
  • Ticks (Lyme is present on this coast): repellent, long pants in brush, full-body check after hikes. Poison oak on sunny edges and river bars.
  • Ocean: sneaker waves and rising tides take lives on this coast — never turn your back, keep kids high on the beach, check tide tables for tidepooling and the Battery Point crossing.
  • River: cold and stronger than it looks even in August; no lifeguards. Bears: all food and toiletries in the metal lockers — it's the law at the campground.
The rules of the ground
  • In the state park, everything is protected: no picking flowers, berries, cones, or mushrooms, no collecting rocks or wood. Cameras yes, baskets no.
  • On adjacent Six Rivers National Forest / Smith River NRA land, some personal gathering is allowed under Forest Service rules — ask at the Gasquet ranger station on US-199 before collecting anything.
  • Dogs: allowed in the campground and on roads/beaches on leash; not on park trails. Drones: prohibited in the parks. Fishing: current CDFW regulations (many Smith runs are catch-and-release, barbless); check before you cast.
  • Stay on the boardwalk at the Grove of Titans, and on trails everywhere — root zones and serpentine soils are the two most easily wounded things in this guide.
