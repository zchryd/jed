Jedediah Smith Redwoods State Park · Del Norte County, California

The river was never theirs to name.
A verified history of the Smith River country.

Eight thousand years of the Tolowa Dee-ni', five June days in 1828, two December floods exactly nine years apart, and the making of a park under the tallest living things on Earth — every date below checked against primary records.

How this was checked: 130 claims were extracted from 26 sources (80 from primary documents — USGS flood reports, the enrolled 1990 statute, Smith and Rogers' 1828 journals, NPS cultural-landscape inventories, Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation publications), then each consolidated fact was adversarially verified by independent reviewers instructed to refute it, with contested figures kept as attributed ranges rather than averaged. Where sources disagree, this report says so.
8000 BP — 1850

The people of Dan'-taa-dvn

Begin at Hiouchi Flat, inside the park, on the terrace above the forks of the river. Radiocarbon dates from sites in and around the park put people here more than 8,000 years ago — among the earliest dates known anywhere in northwest California. On that flat stood the Tolowa village Chvn-su'lh-dvn, “Red Elderberry Place,” where archaeologists excavated the earliest known redwood plank house in northwestern California, built about 1,267 years ago — and where the record runs continuously from ancient camps to a sweathouse with square nails and shotgun shells, occupied into the early 1900s. Hiouchi itself carries the Tolowa name Xaa-yuu-chit.

This is the heart of Taa-laa-waa-dvn, the aboriginal territory of the Tolowa Dee-ni' — roughly Wilson Creek in the south to the Sixes River in Oregon, the ocean horizon to the Applegate watershed, about 2.87 million acres organized into districts called yvtlh-'i~. Del Norte County is Dan'-taa-dvn. The river now called the Smith is Xaa-wvn-taa-ghii~-li~. Three villages anchored the California districts: Taa-'at-dvn at Crescent Bay, 'Ee-chuu-le' at the Lake Earl lagoon, and Yan'-daa-k'vt — Yontocket — near the river's mouth, remembered as the Center of the Tolowa World, where peoples gathered at the solstices for Nee-dash, the world-renewal dance.

The river fed all of it. Salmon bone rises sharply in the archaeological record about 1,250 years ago, alongside the first specialized gear — net weights, harpoon tips. When the first Americans walked through in 1828, Tolowa traders met them in canoes with lamprey eels to sell. The fishery is not background to this history; it is the history.

JUNE 1828

Five days that named the river

Jedediah Strong Smith — already the first American to enter California overland — left the Sacramento Valley in spring 1828 trying to reach Oregon. At Red Bluff on April 10, 1828 he gave up on an eastward Sierra crossing and turned his brigade — Smith plus 17–19 hired men, driving some 300 horses and mules — west and north. On June 8 they struck the Pacific, at a landfall generally identified with the mouth of Wilson Creek near Requa, in today's Del Norte County, and began picking their way up the coast through Tolowa country.

On June 19, 1828, from a camp hemmed in by the lakes and sloughs near Lake Earl, Smith scouted ahead with two men and found a river about eighty yards wide emptying into a broad bay, crossing and recrossing it “with great difficulty” — the first recorded European-American crossing of the river that would carry his name. On June 20 the whole party forded it — clerk Harrison Rogers logged it at 60 to 70 yards wide and “deep fording” — and camped in a bottom prairie, where about twenty Tolowa people arrived by canoe to trade lamprey eels for beads. On June 22, working back toward the sea, Rogers wrote down trees “from 10 to 15 feet in diameter” — one of the earliest written descriptions of redwood-scale forest on this coast, though he took the giants for hemlock and cedar. On June 23 they crossed the Winchuck into Oregon. Five days, and this landscape had entered the written record.

The party included the first non-Native people known to have walked these redwood forests — among them Peter Ranne, a Black man — and the first horses and mules ever brought through them. Three weeks later, on July 14, 1828, nearly all of them were dead: Kelawatset (Lower Umpqua) men attacked the camp on the Umpqua River while Smith and two others were away scouting, killing about 15 of the party, Rogers among them. A Hudson's Bay Company detail buried eleven bodies at the site on October 28 and recovered, with the rest of the property, Rogers' journals — the only reason the day-by-day record survives. Smith never named the river after himself; his journals never name it at all. Others attached his name to it after a Comanche party killed him on the Cimarron on May 27, 1831.

Worth knowing: Smith's route crossed the river near its mouth and turned north along the coast. He never traveled upstream into the redwood forks where the park that bears his name now stands — the party's only documented brush with the big trees is Rogers' astonished diary line of June 22.
1851 — 1868

The catastrophe

This section concerns massacres of Tolowa Dee-ni' people. Death tolls are given as the sources give them — as attributed ranges, never averaged — following the practice of the historians and of the Nation, whose members' ancestors survived these events.

Gold found on the Trinity and Klamath brought American settlement crashing into Dan'-taa-dvn in the early 1850s: Crescent City was laid out on the village lands of Taa-'at-dvn in 1853. That fall came the event around which everything after turns. During the ten-night Nee-dash gathering at Yontocket — held after the fall harvest, with Tolowa, Chetco, Pistol River, Tututni and Rogue River people assembled — a militia of roughly 30–33 men raised in Crescent City surrounded the village on the third night and attacked at dawn, burning it to the ground.

Figures contested — reported as attributed ranges Yontocket, fall 1853: settler-derived “official” estimates say about 150 killed and are widely regarded as understated; a Tolowa witness put the dead above 450; Tolowa accounts say as many as 600. Bodies burned or lost in the slough made a full count impossible. However counted, it stands among the deadliest massacres in California history. Accounts that date it “spring 1853” conflate the massacre with the departure of a prospecting party earlier that year; the scholarship (Madley 2016; Norton 1979) supports the after-harvest Nee-dash dating. No perpetrator was ever prosecuted. The burned village became known on land records as “Burnt Ranch.”

Survivors moved north to Howonquet at the river's mouth. The violence followed them. In 1854, settlers attacked 'Ee-chuu-le' (Achulet) beside Lake Earl at dawn, on the pretext of a suspected horse theft.

Figures contested — reported as attributed ranges Achulet, 1854: the attackers' own count was 65 killed — a figure that excluded people whose bodies sank in Lake Earl, and is therefore a floor; historians' estimates run as high as 150. No month or day is reliably documented. Settlers later renamed the site “Pay Way,” after Old Pay Way, one of the few survivors. An 1855 attack at Howonquet killed about 70 more; a raid at Chetko killed about 24. One synthesis (Norton, 1979) counts over 900 Tolowa killed across roughly seven years — historians treat that total as an attributed estimate, but the individual events above are independently corroborated.

In 1862 the federal government established the Smith River Reservation in the county's northwest corner — its reported size ranges from about 17,000 acres (Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation) to 44,000 acres (county historical society) — together with Camp Lincoln, an Army post northeast of Crescent City whose stated purpose sources render both ways: protecting the Tolowa from settler violence, and controlling them. The reservation lasted six years. After the 1866 death of the Xaa-wan'-k'wvt headman K'ay-lish, it was discontinued on July 28, 1868, and its people pushed into a chain of removals already underway — to the Klamath River Reservation (est. 1855) and, after the Rogue River Wars, north to Oregon reservations including Siletz, a multi-stage forced relocation spanning roughly 1856–1862. One county account counts 1,834 people removed to Oregon, from a pre-contact Dee-ni' population that same account puts above 10,000 (scholarly estimates vary widely). Tolowa families nonetheless remained, returned, and stayed — the through-line the last section of this report picks up.

1865 — 1892

Wrecks, lights, and roads

On July 30, 1865, the overloaded sidewheeler Brother Jonathan, carrying an Army payroll in gold, turned back toward Crescent City in a gale and struck Dragon Rocks on St. George Reef, four miles off Point St. George. Nineteen people got away in a single lifeboat. More than two hundred died — commonly cited as 225 of 244 aboard, the deadliest shipwreck on the Pacific Coast to that time. Twenty-eight headstones still stand in the Brother Jonathan Cemetery on the 9th Street bluffs. The wreck was located in 1993 and the gold partly recovered in 1996. The disaster forced the building of St. George Reef Lighthouse on the reef itself — a decade of work and roughly $700,000, among the costliest lighthouses ever built in America — first lit on October 20, 1892. (Local accounts saying 1891 date the structure's completion, not the light.)

Inland, the roads tell the settlement story. The Cold Springs Mountain Trail was carrying pack trains from Crescent City toward the Oregon mines by 1853, over Howland Hill and across the Smith at Catchings Ferry; Ben Kelsey's 1855 trail ran just south. But Howland Hill Road itself — today's famous drive through the big trees — is not the 1850s toll road the guidebooks describe: it was surveyed in 1881 by J. S. Howard and built in 1887 by the Christensen brothers as a free county road connecting Crescent City to Horace Gasquet's 1881 toll road. When the Redwood Highway pushed through (Gasquet–Kerby segment in 1926, the Hiouchi Bridge finishing the bypass in June 1929), the old road fell quiet — which is exactly why it survives as a narrow lane among giants.

1929 — 1949

Making the park

The park began as a memorial. In 1927, lumberman Frank D. Stout — president of the Del Norte Lumber Company — opened negotiations with Save the Redwoods League to donate a grove at the Mill Creek–Smith River confluence in memory of his late son Henry. Stout died before completing the gift; his widow Clara finished it in 1929, naming the roughly 44-acre stand the Frank D. Stout Memorial Grove. It became the first dedicated grove of the new park: 300-foot trees that were never logged, at the exact junction of creek and river.

The Civilian Conservation Corps built the visitor's park in the 1930s: Company 1903, working out of Camp Prairie Creek, graded the entrance road, raised a custodian's residence, and built the riverside campground — tables, stoves, cupboards, restrooms — plus a footbridge across the Smith to Stout Grove. The river's floods long ago took that bridge; today's summer-only footbridge (installed each July–September as water levels allow) is its successor in spirit, on the same crossing to the same grove.

The largest single addition came as a war memorial. From a 1939 League agreement to buy 500 acres of old growth a year from the Del Norte Lumber Company, through the Garden Club of America's May 1944 proposal, to a final Daughters-of-the-American-Revolution-funded purchase in 1949, the National Tribute Grove — more than 5,000 acres along Howland Hill Road, boundaries surveyed by Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. — was dedicated in 1949 to Americans who served in the Second World War, completing public ownership along the old road. Smaller dedications thread the same years: Metcalf Grove (1942), the Simpson-Reed Grove, and the Boy Scout Tree, found by county sheriff and Scout-troop founder Jack Breen, whose Mill Creek bridge on Howland Hill Road was dedicated to him by the Scouts in 1950.

The name: the park established in 1929 took Jedediah Smith's name for the 1828 passage — a park he never saw, on a river he crossed once, near its mouth, and never named. Since 1994 its 10,000 acres have been managed jointly by California State Parks and the National Park Service as part of Redwood National and State Parks, which together protect 45 percent of California's remaining old-growth redwood.
1931 — 1990

The undammed river, measured

The Smith is the only major river system in California that flows entirely undammed, headwaters to sea — the National Wild & Scenic Rivers program says it plainly, and the California Legislature put it in a 2015 resolution. Mainstem gauging began in 1931 at the station just downstream of the park, and twice recorded catastrophe on the same calendar day:

  • Dec 22 1955Flood crest at 3:30 a.m. — 165,000 cfs, the record to date. About 7,600 acres of the delta went under an average of three feet of water; flood warnings kept cattle losses low.
  • Dec 22 1964The “Christmas flood” — 228,000 cfs, 138% of 1955, destroying the gauging station itself. USGS judged it probably equal to or greater than the legendary winter flood of 1861–62. Fifteen miles of US-199 wrecked in the Middle Fork canyon with three bridges; the US-101 river bridge damaged; ~9,300 delta acres flooded; Crescent City's water supply cut until January 4 — and not one life lost in the Smith basin.

The same year 1964 had already broken Crescent City another way: the tsunami from the great Alaska earthquake arrived in the early hours of March 28, killing 11 people (accounts range 10–13), destroying or damaging about 29 blocks, and doing over $14 million in damage — the deadliest tsunami event recorded on the U.S. West Coast. (The written tsunami record here opens with the January 1700 Cascadia megathrust; the most recent damaging surge came from the July 2025 Kamchatka earthquake.)

Protection came in two acts: on January 19, 1981, Interior Secretary Cecil Andrus designated the Smith a federal Wild & Scenic River at California's request — and on November 16, 1990, the Smith River National Recreation Area Act (P.L. 101-612) wrote more than 300 miles of the mainstem, forks, and tributaries into the Wild & Scenic system by statute and created the 450-square-mile NRA under Six Rivers National Forest. (The printed statute says the earlier designation happened in “January 1990” — a typographical error; the Federal Register citation it gives, 46 Fed. Reg. 7483, is from 1981.) The river remains a stronghold for threatened coho, Chinook, and steelhead — the same runs the plank-house builders were netting a thousand years ago.

1998 — TODAY

Loved nearly to death, and tended again

The park's newest history is about carrying capacity — and return. When GPS coordinates for the Grove of Titans, an off-trail stand of colossal redwoods above Mill Creek, spread online around 2011, years of trampling followed: a web of social trails that crushed understory, compacted the giants' shallow root systems, and bled soil into coho streams. The repair — planned from 2018 by State Parks, Save the Redwoods League, the Redwood Parks Conservancy and the NPS, with the Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation consulting — realigned the three-mile Mill Creek Trail and raised a 1,300-foot elevated boardwalk through the grove, opened May 2022: $4 million, 23,000 crew hours, 128 tons of material carried in by hand. The interpretive panels teach the Tolowa word for redwood: K'vsh-chu.

The Nation itself — more than 2,000 enrolled citizens, 950+ acres of tribal land — is again shaping the land directly. On October 21, 2025, Tolowa Dee-ni' fire practitioners and California State Parks returned cultural burning to See-tr'ee-ghin-dvm-dvn within the park: good fire, back in a forest that grew up with it, lit by the people who have been here for the whole eight thousand years this report covers.

CHECKED

Campfire claims, checked against the record

“Return of the Jedi filmed the Endor scenes here in the park.”
No confirmed filming inside Jedediah Smith Redwoods State Park (medium confidence — the best sources are expert fan research summarized by Save the Redwoods League). The Endor sets were mainly on private timberland near the town of Smith River — since logged — with speeder-bike footage from Cheatham Grove in Grizzly Creek Redwoods State Park and the Avenue of the Giants, a hundred miles south.
“Howland Hill Road is the old 1850s stagecoach toll road.”
Surveyed 1881, built 1887 as a free county road. The 1850s kernel: it traces earlier pack trails (Cold Springs Mountain Trail, by 1853; Kelsey Trail, 1855) — horse paths, never wagon roads.
“St. George Reef Light first shone in 1891.”
First lit October 20, 1892. The 1891 date describes the near-complete structure, not the light.
“The 1964 flood was the biggest ever.”
Biggest since gauging began in 1931 — and USGS judged it “probably equaled or exceeded” the 1861–62 flood, which predates the instruments. Respect the fine print.
